Two variables are correlated. Which one causes the other? Without an experiment, the direction is not in the data unless you assume something about the mechanism.

In February I posted a preprint [\[1\]](#ref-1): train one network to predict $$Y$$ from $$X$$, another to predict $$X$$ from $$Y$$, and the one that converges faster is the causal direction. I then ran the experiment properly. The direction is decided by which network reaches the *lower* loss, not by which gets there *sooner*. The anticausal network is usually the faster one. It loses only when it cannot get low enough.

Below: the theory that does hold, the 360 runs behind that statement, the Tübingen benchmark under its standard protocol, and where this sits in the 2024–2026 literature. The script is linked at the end.

## Why correlation cannot fix the arrow

![Three causal structures with identical correlation](/assets/img/cca/three_configs.png)
_Figure 1. Three data-generating structures that produce the same correlation between $$X$$ and $$Y$$. Nothing in the second moments of the joint distribution separates them._

Pearl's hierarchy [\[2\]](#ref-2) has three rungs. Rung one is association, $$P(Y \mid X = x)$$. Rung two is intervention, $$P(Y \mid \mathrm{do}(X = x))$$, where you force $$X$$ to $$x$$ and sever every arrow into it. Rung three is counterfactual. Bareinboim, Correa, Ibeling and Icard proved in 2022 that outside a measure-zero set of distributions, a lower rung cannot answer a higher rung's question [\[3\]](#ref-3). Every model trained on observational data lives on rung one, language models included. The do-calculus is complete [\[4\]](#ref-4): given the graph, if its three rules cannot reduce an interventional query to an observational one, nothing can. But it needs the graph, and a graph starts with the direction of each edge.

Without a further assumption the bivariate direction is unanswerable: every joint density factorizes both as $$p(x)\,p(y \mid x)$$ and as $$p(y)\,p(x \mid y)$$. The assumption that breaks the tie is a restriction on what kind of mechanism nature is allowed to use.

## The additive noise model and why direction becomes identifiable

> **Definition 1 (Additive noise model).** $$X$$ and $$Y$$ follow an ANM in the direction $$X \to Y$$ if
>
> $$
> Y = f(X) + \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \perp X, \qquad E[\varepsilon] = 0,\ \mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 < \infty.
> \tag{1}
> $$

Independence is the load-bearing word. $$\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon, X) = 0$$ says there is no linear relationship. $$\varepsilon \perp X$$ says the joint density factorizes, so no function of $$X$$ carries information about $$\varepsilon$$. The standard example separating the two: $$X \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$ and $$W = X^2$$ have $$\mathrm{Cov}(X, W) = E[X^3] = 0$$, yet $$W$$ determines $$X$$ up to sign. It comes back in Section 5.

> **Theorem 1 (Identifiability of nonlinear ANMs; Hoyer et al. 2009, Peters et al. 2014).** Suppose $$(X, Y)$$ admits an ANM $$X \to Y$$ with $$f$$ nonlinear and three times differentiable, and $$p_X$$, $$p_\varepsilon$$ strictly positive and three times differentiable. Then, except for a set of $$(f, p_X, p_\varepsilon)$$ triples characterized by a specific differential equation, there is no $$g$$ and no $$\eta \perp Y$$ with $$X = g(Y) + \eta$$. [\[5\]](#ref-5), [\[6\]](#ref-6)

The exceptional set is essentially the linear-Gaussian case. There the reverse ANM always exists: if $$Y = aX + \varepsilon$$ with $$X$$ and $$\varepsilon$$ jointly Gaussian, then $$X = bY + \eta$$ with $$\eta \perp Y$$, because for jointly Gaussian variables uncorrelated means independent. Darmois [\[7\]](#ref-7) and Skitovich [\[8\]](#ref-8) proved the converse in 1953: if two linear combinations of independent variables are independent, the shared variables are Gaussian. So a linear ANM is bidirectional if and only if the noise is Gaussian. That is why LiNGAM [\[9\]](#ref-9) works with non-Gaussian noise, why Peters and Bühlmann needed equal error variances to recover identifiability for Gaussian SEMs [\[10\]](#ref-10), and why a fresh minimax analysis from August 2026 finds the sample complexity of bivariate LiNGAM blows up as the disturbances approach Gaussianity, with the exact law $$N^\star \asymp \log(1/\delta)\,/\,(d_\beta^2 + \beta^2 \nu^2)$$ where $$\nu$$ measures the distance from Gaussian [\[11\]](#ref-11).

Theorem 1 says a direction exists in the distribution, not how to read it. Every bivariate method reads a different statistic of the same asymmetry; Section 9 lists them.

## The toolkit

Five facts do the work. Each is stated once and used later.

### The conditional mean is the MSE-optimal predictor

For any measurable $$g$$, write $$g(X) - Y = (g(X) - E[Y \mid X]) + (E[Y \mid X] - Y)$$. Square, take expectations; the cross term vanishes by the tower property $$E[\phi(X)\,(Y - E[Y \mid X])] = 0$$ for every $$\phi$$:

$$
E\big[(g(X) - Y)^2\big] = E\big[(g(X) - E[Y \mid X])^2\big] + E\big[\mathrm{Var}(Y \mid X)\big].
\tag{2}
$$

The second term does not depend on $$g$$. The population MSE is minimized by $$g^*(X) = E[Y \mid X]$$ and the minimum, the **loss floor**, is $$\mathcal{L}^* = E[\mathrm{Var}(Y \mid X)]$$. No network of any size gets below it.

### The law of total variance

$$
\mathrm{Var}(X) = E\big[\mathrm{Var}(X \mid Y)\big] + \mathrm{Var}\big(E[X \mid Y]\big).
\tag{3}
$$

Total spread equals the spread left after seeing $$Y$$ plus the spread of the best prediction. By (2), the first term is the loss floor for predicting $$X$$ from $$Y$$.

### The Polyak–Łojasiewicz condition and the gradient-descent rate

A loss $$\mathcal{L}$$ with minimum $$\mathcal{L}^*$$ satisfies PL with constant $$\mu > 0$$ if

$$
\tfrac{1}{2}\,\|\nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta)\|^2 \;\geq\; \mu\,\big(\mathcal{L}(\theta) - \mathcal{L}^*\big) \quad \text{for all } \theta.
\tag{4}
$$

It is weaker than convexity and holds for overparameterized networks near their minima under mild conditions. If $$\mathcal{L}$$ is $$L$$-smooth and gradient descent uses $$\eta \leq 1/L$$, the descent lemma plus (4) gives the linear rate of Karimi, Nutini and Schmidt [\[12\]](#ref-12):

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta_t) - \mathcal{L}^* \;\leq\; (1 - \eta\mu)^t\,\big(\mathcal{L}(\theta_0) - \mathcal{L}^*\big).
\tag{5}
$$

With stochastic gradients of variance $$\sigma_g^2$$ there is an additive floor that does not decay (Bottou, Curtis and Nocedal, Theorem 4.6 [\[13\]](#ref-13)):

$$
E\big[\mathcal{L}(\theta_t) - \mathcal{L}^*\big] \;\leq\; (1 - \eta\mu)^t\,\big(\mathcal{L}_0 - \mathcal{L}^*\big) + \frac{\eta L \sigma_g^2}{2\mu}.
\tag{6}
$$

### Steps to reach a threshold

Set the right side of (5) to a target $$\tau > \mathcal{L}^*$$ and solve:

$$
T(\tau) \;\approx\; \frac{1}{\eta\mu}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}_0 - \mathcal{L}^*}{\tau - \mathcal{L}^*}\right), \qquad T(\tau) = \infty \ \text{ if } \tau \leq \mathcal{L}^*.
\tag{7}
$$

Two things to notice. The count is infinite if the threshold is below the floor. And, with $$\eta$$, $$\mu$$, $$\mathcal{L}_0$$, $$\tau$$ fixed, $$\partial T / \partial \mathcal{L}^* = \frac{1}{\eta\mu}\big[\frac{1}{\tau - \mathcal{L}^*} - \frac{1}{\mathcal{L}_0 - \mathcal{L}^*}\big] > 0$$. A higher floor costs more steps if all else is equal. Section 6 tests whether it is.

### z-scoring

Standardize both variables before training. After that, an MSE of $$\tau$$ means "the unexplained fraction of variance is $$\tau$$" in either direction. Without it the threshold is in different units. For $$Y = X^3 + \varepsilon$$ with $$X \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$, Wick's theorem gives $$E[X^6] = 5!! = 15$$, so $$\mathrm{Var}(Y) \approx 15$$ against $$\mathrm{Var}(X) = 1$$; a raw $$\tau = 0.05$$ asks the forward network for 99.7% of the variance and the reverse for 95%. The same concern, under the name varsortability, is why simulated causal benchmarks can be gamed by variance ordering [\[14\]](#ref-14) and why the newest bivariate papers standardize inside the data-generating process itself [\[15\]](#ref-15).

## The idea

![CCA pipeline](/assets/img/cca/cca_pipeline.png)
_Figure 2. The procedure. Both networks share architecture, optimizer, seed, threshold and step cap; only the direction differs._

> **Definition 2 (CCA score).** Let $$T_{\mathrm{fwd}}$$ be the number of optimizer steps until the held-out MSE of $$g_\theta : \tilde{X} \to \tilde{Y}$$ first drops below $$\tau$$, and $$T_{\mathrm{rev}}$$ the same for $$h_\phi : \tilde{Y} \to \tilde{X}$$, both capped at $$T_{\max}$$. Then
>
> $$
> \mathrm{CCA}(X \to Y) \;=\; T_{\mathrm{fwd}} - T_{\mathrm{rev}}.
> \tag{8}
> $$
>
> Negative predicts $$X \to Y$$, positive predicts $$Y \to X$$, zero is an abstention.

![Causal graph and reverse](/assets/img/cca/fig1_graph.png)
_Figure 3a. From the preprint: the causal structure and the harder reverse problem._

![Schematic of the convergence gap](/assets/img/cca/fig1_convergence.png)
_Figure 3b. From the preprint: the expected signal. The forward loss crosses $$\tau$$ at $$T_{\mathrm{fwd}}$$; the reverse plateaus above $$\tau$$. Compare with Figure 4._

The intuition is older than the preprint. Schölkopf and colleagues argued in 2012 that learning is easier in the causal direction [\[16\]](#ref-16). Bengio and colleagues conjectured in 2019 that the causal model *adapts fastest* under distribution shift [\[17\]](#ref-17). The step from "easier" to "fewer gradient steps" is the one that fails.

## What is actually asymmetric

The preprint chained three lemmas: reverse residuals stay correlated with the input (Lemma 1); that makes the reverse landscape harder (Lemma 2); harder landscapes cost more steps under PL (Lemma 3). Two of the three need restating.

### Lemma 1, corrected: the asymmetry is in the residual's distribution, not its covariance

The preprint argued that for any finite-capacity reverse network $$h_\phi \neq h^*$$, with $$\delta_\phi(Y) = h_\phi(Y) - h^*(Y)$$, one gets $$\mathrm{Cov}(R_{\mathrm{rev}}, Y) = -\mathrm{Cov}(\delta_\phi(Y), Y) \neq 0$$ "because a non-constant function of $$Y$$ has nonzero covariance with $$Y$$". That inference is false; $$\delta_\phi(Y) = Y^2$$ with symmetric $$Y$$ is the counterexample from Section 2. And the identical argument applies to the forward direction at any finite approximation, since $$R_{\mathrm{fwd}} = \varepsilon + (f - g_\theta)(X)$$. The lemma, even if it were true, would establish no asymmetry.

The asymmetry that exists lives at the population optimum and in the full distribution of the residual.

> **Lemma 1 (Residual structure at the optimum).** Under the ANM (1) with nonlinear $$f$$:
>
> 1. The forward optimum is $$g^* = f$$ and its residual $$R^*_{\mathrm{fwd}} = \varepsilon$$ is **independent** of $$X$$.
> 2. The reverse optimum is $$h^*(Y) = E[X \mid Y]$$ and its residual $$R^*_{\mathrm{rev}} = X - E[X \mid Y]$$ is **mean-independent** of $$Y$$ ($$E[R^*_{\mathrm{rev}} \mid Y] = 0$$, hence uncorrelated with every $$\phi(Y)$$) but generically **not independent** of $$Y$$: $$\mathrm{Var}(R^*_{\mathrm{rev}} \mid Y = y) = \mathrm{Var}(X \mid Y = y)$$ depends on $$y$$.
>
> *Proof.* (1) is immediate from (1). For (2), mean-independence is the tower property; the conditional variance is constant in $$y$$ only when the conditional law of $$X$$ given $$Y$$ is a location family, which for strictly positive densities forces $$f$$ affine and the pair jointly Gaussian, the exceptional set of Theorem 1. $$\square$$

In the causal direction the residual is pure noise and any independence test passes. In the anticausal direction the residual is uncorrelated with everything, but its *spread* tracks $$Y$$. A covariance test cannot see that; a kernel test like HSIC can, which is why RESIT [\[6\]](#ref-6) uses one. Where does the varying spread come from? Linearize $$f$$ near $$x_0 = f^{-1}(y)$$ where $$f'$$ is bounded away from zero:

$$
\mathrm{Var}(X \mid Y = y) \;\approx\; \frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{f'(f^{-1}(y))^2}.
\tag{9}
$$

Steep mechanism, tight reverse conditional; flat mechanism, wide one. It fails where $$f' \to 0$$, which is exactly where the reverse problem is hardest, so it explains the shape but not the number. For the number, compute the floor.

### Lemma 2, corrected: loss floors, and when the reverse one is higher

After z-scoring, (2) and (3) give both floors in comparable units:

$$
\mathcal{L}^*_{\mathrm{fwd}} = \frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}, \qquad
\mathcal{L}^*_{\mathrm{rev}} = \frac{E[\mathrm{Var}(X \mid Y)]}{\mathrm{Var}(X)} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{Var}(E[X \mid Y])}{\mathrm{Var}(X)}.
\tag{10}
$$

The preprint asserted $$\mathcal{L}^*_{\mathrm{rev}} > \mathcal{L}^*_{\mathrm{fwd}}$$ for nonlinear injective $$f$$; its proof showed only $$\mathcal{L}^*_{\mathrm{rev}} < 1$$. The comparison is the subject of a 2018 theorem by Blöbaum, Janzing, Washio, Shimizu and Schölkopf, the method known as RECI.

> **Theorem 2 (Error asymmetry; Blöbaum et al. 2018).** Let the ANM (1) hold with $$f$$ monotone and twice differentiable, write $$\tilde{E}_\alpha$$ for the effect at noise scale $$\alpha$$, and assume an *independence of cause and mechanism* condition that decorrelates $$f'^2$$ from the cause density. Then
>
> $$
> \lim_{\alpha \to 0}\ \frac{E[\mathrm{Var}(C \mid \tilde{E}_\alpha)]}{E[\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{E}_\alpha \mid C)]} \;\geq\; 1,
> \tag{11}
> $$
>
> with equality only if $$f$$ is linear. [\[18\]](#ref-18)

The anticausal regression error dominates the causal one in the small-noise limit, under an independence condition, with equality in the linear case. At large noise it can fail. This is the floor asymmetry my Lemma 2 needed, and a convergence-time criterion inherits its conditions.

### Lemma 3, corrected: floor to steps, and the assumption that carries the weight

Put (10) into (7). Under PL with the *same* $$\mu$$, the same $$\eta$$ and the same initial loss in both directions,

$$
T_{\mathrm{dir}} \;\approx\; \frac{1}{\eta\mu}\ln\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}_0 - \mathcal{L}^*_{\mathrm{dir}}}{\tau - \mathcal{L}^*_{\mathrm{dir}}}\right), \qquad \mathrm{dir} \in \{\mathrm{fwd}, \mathrm{rev}\},
\tag{12}
$$

and there are two regimes.

**Regime A: the threshold separates the floors.** If $$\mathcal{L}^*_{\mathrm{fwd}} < \tau \leq \mathcal{L}^*_{\mathrm{rev}}$$, then $$T_{\mathrm{fwd}}$$ is finite and $$T_{\mathrm{rev}} = T_{\max}$$. The score is strongly negative and its magnitude means only "the reverse never got there". CCA is then a thresholded RECI: it asks which direction's error can get below $$\tau$$, and the optimizer is irrelevant.

**Regime B: both floors are below the threshold.** Both networks converge and the comparison is about speed. From (12), $$T_{\mathrm{rev}} > T_{\mathrm{fwd}}$$ iff the reverse floor is higher, *given equal PL constants*. This is the only regime where convergence time adds anything to a floor comparison, and the only one where the equal-$$\mu$$ assumption cannot be dodged.

> **Proposition 1 (Conditional convergence-time asymmetry).** Suppose (i) the ANM (1) holds with $$f$$ nonlinear; (ii) $$\mathcal{L}^*_{\mathrm{fwd}} < \mathcal{L}^*_{\mathrm{rev}}$$, for instance under the conditions of Theorem 2; (iii) both objectives satisfy PL near their minima with the same $$\mu$$, are optimized with the same step size, and start from comparable initial loss. Then for every $$\tau > \mathcal{L}^*_{\mathrm{fwd}}$$,
>
> $$
> E[T_{\mathrm{fwd}}] \;<\; E[T_{\mathrm{rev}}].
> \tag{13}
> $$
>
> *Proof.* If $$\tau \leq \mathcal{L}^*_{\mathrm{rev}}$$ this is Regime A. Otherwise both counts are given by (12) and the monotonicity after (7) gives the strict inequality. With stochastic gradients replace each floor by $$\mathcal{L}^* + \eta L \sigma_g^2 / 2\mu$$ from (6); the argument is unchanged if the noise floors are equal, which (iii) includes. $$\square$$

Assumption (ii) is RECI. Assumption (iii) is where the preprint's "$$\Omega(1/\eta\mu)$$ gap" came from, and it cannot be verified from outside: the PL constant of a network's loss is a property of the landscape you are in the middle of exploring. Le Priol, Babanezhad, Bengio and Lacoste-Julien had already found, analyzing adaptation speed in 2021, situations "where the anticausal model is advantaged, falsifying the initial hypothesis" [\[19\]](#ref-19). So the question is not whether (13) can be proved but whether (iii) holds, and that is an experiment.

## The experiment: 360 runs, recorded step by step

### Design

Six mechanisms, $$X \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$, Gaussian noise at $$\sigma_\varepsilon \in \{0.1, 0.316, 1.0\}$$, ten seeds each, $$n = 1000$$ with an 80/20 held-out split, both variables z-scored with training statistics. MLP-64-64 with tanh, Adam [\[20\]](#ref-20) at $$10^{-3}$$, batch 128, 3000 steps, the preprint's configuration. Both directions of a run share the seed and hence the initialization. One design decision: I record the held-out MSE every five steps and keep the whole trajectory, so *any* threshold rule can be evaluated afterwards from the same training runs. The 360 networks are trained as one stacked model with one Adam instance, which is mathematically identical to 360 separate runs because Adam is elementwise and the losses are independent; it takes three minutes on a laptop CPU.

### The rule as a function of the threshold

![Decision of the threshold rule as tau moves](/assets/img/cca/exp_tau_sweep.png)
_Figure 5. This explains the preprint's synthetic results. The correct answers live in the window between the two floors, Regime A. Below the forward floor nobody converges and the rule abstains. Above the reverse floor the rule enters Regime B, and there it is *mostly wrong* on the exponential and cubic mechanisms: the anticausal network reaches a loose threshold first. The value $$\tau = 0.05$$ happens to fall inside the correct window for four of the five mechanisms, which is why the preprint saw 30 out of 30 on sine and exponential. Move the threshold and the answer moves.

Three findings.

**The floor comparison is the signal.** Which direction reaches *lower* is right on every seed of every nonlinear mechanism at low and moderate noise, and degrades at $$\sigma_\varepsilon = 1.0$$ exactly where Theorem 2 stops applying. That is RECI, computed with neural networks instead of low-degree regressors.

**The anticausal direction is usually faster.** By $$t_{90}$$, the effect-to-cause network wins in 78 to 100 percent of seeds on sine, exponential and cubic at every noise level. The one exception is $$\tanh 2X$$ at low noise. The reverse target $$E[X \mid Y]$$ is a smoother, lower-amplitude function than $$f$$ on these mechanisms, and networks fit low-frequency structure first, the spectral bias documented by Rahaman et al. [\[21\]](#ref-21). So assumption (iii) of Proposition 1 fails, in the direction that breaks the theorem: the reverse landscape is *better* conditioned. The preprint's Experiment 4 had already measured this: the gradient-norm variance ratio, reverse over forward, was 0.376 and 0.054 on the cubic, 0.684 and 0.276 on the exponential, 0.730 and 0.608 on $$X^2$$ at initialization and mid-training, above 1 only on the sine. The preprint dismissed the ratio as an unreliable proxy. It was measuring the conditioning correctly.

**The threshold rule works only where the reverse cannot follow.** Every correct answer the preprint's rule gives in Table 2 is a Regime A answer: the threshold sits above the causal floor and below the anticausal one. The rule ties on every seed once the noise pushes both floors above $$\tau$$, which is what "$$\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.1)$$" read as a variance would have done to the preprint's sine and exponential results. Those results are only consistent with $$\sigma_\varepsilon = 0.1$$ as a standard deviation.

## Boundary conditions

### Linear Gaussian

Both floors are equal (Table 1), both networks converge at the same rate (Figure 4), and the score is noise around zero. The preprint reported $$\mathrm{CCA} < 0$$ in only 1 of 30 seeds. Under a symmetric null you expect about 15. A tilt that large means something in that pipeline favored one direction when the data did not; a shared random-number stream with the forward network trained first would do it. My batched runs, which draw both directions' minibatches from one generator, give 4 of 10, consistent with chance.

![Boundary conditions from the preprint](/assets/img/cca/fig_boundary_conditions.png)
_Figure 10. From the preprint. Left: linear Gaussian, scores concentrated near zero. Right: $$X^2$$, scores of $$-3000$$ in 29 of 30 seeds. Under Definition 2 a score of $$-3000$$ predicts $$X \to Y$$, the correct direction; the preprint's text and its Table 6 disagree with its own figure about the sign._

### Non-injective mechanisms

The preprint reported that on $$Y = X^2 + \varepsilon$$ the reverse network "converges in 8 to 25 steps" by learning the constant zero while the forward needs the full cap, giving a confidently wrong answer:

| Seed | $$T_{\mathrm{fwd}}$$ | $$T_{\mathrm{rev}}$$ | Reported |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1000 | 9 | wrong |
| 1 | 1000 | 8 | wrong |
| 2 | 1000 | 20 | wrong |
| 3 | 1000 | 21 | wrong |
| 4 | 1000 | 9 | wrong |
| 5 | 1000 | 22 | wrong |
| 6 | 1000 | 25 | wrong |
| 7 | 1000 | 8 | wrong |
| 8 | 1000 | 23 | wrong |
| 9 | 1000 | 8 | wrong |

_Table 5. From the preprint: the $$X^2$$ boundary condition, ten seeds._

Table 1 makes this impossible under the stated stopping rule. The reverse floor for $$X^2$$ is $$1.0$$; a network predicting $$X$$ from $$Y$$ cannot get its held-out MSE below $$1.0$$, let alone $$0.05$$. Under "stop when held-out MSE $$< \tau$$", the reverse hits the cap, the forward converges (floor $$0.005$$), and the score predicts the *correct* direction. My runs confirm it: 10 of 10 correct at $$\sigma_\varepsilon = 0.1$$ (Table 2), and Figure 4's red line for $$X^2$$ never leaves $$1.0$$. The only way to obtain $$T_{\mathrm{rev}} \approx 10$$ is a stopping rule that fires when the loss *stops improving*, which it does immediately when the optimum is a constant. So the preprint's code used a plateau criterion in that experiment, and the "degenerate collapse" belongs to the criterion, not the method. With a threshold rule, non-injective mechanisms become correct answers or abstentions, never confident errors. Non-injectivity remains a limit of ANM identifiability, Peters et al.'s Proposition 23 [\[6\]](#ref-6). It should cost a decision, not produce a wrong one.

## Where convergence time sits among the alternatives

Every method below estimates the asymmetry of Theorem 1 from a different statistic.

| Method | Statistic read from the data | Key assumption |
|---|---|---|
| LiNGAM [\[9\]](#ref-9) | Non-Gaussianity of linear residuals | Linear mechanism, non-Gaussian noise |
| RESIT / ANM-HSIC [\[5\]](#ref-5), [\[6\]](#ref-6) | Independence of residual and input | Nonlinear ANM |
| Post-nonlinear model [\[22\]](#ref-22) | Residual independence after an outer invertible transform | PNL model |
| IGCI [\[23\]](#ref-23), [\[24\]](#ref-24) | Entropy or slope-based description length | Near-deterministic mechanism |
| RECI [\[18\]](#ref-18) | Ratio of regression MSEs, equation (10) | ANM, independent mechanism, small noise |
| SLOPE, RDMDL [\[25\]](#ref-25), [\[26\]](#ref-26) | MDL of the mechanism, and of the cause | Algorithmic independence of cause and mechanism |
| CGNN [\[27\]](#ref-27) | MMD between generated and observed joint, per direction | Generative neural causal model |
| bQCD, QPE, LRQS [\[28\]](#ref-28), [\[15\]](#ref-15), [\[29\]](#ref-29) | Shape of conditional quantiles across levels | Location-scale or low-rank quantile structure |
| LOCI, CAREFL, ROCHE [\[30\]](#ref-30), [\[31\]](#ref-31), [\[32\]](#ref-32) | Likelihood of a heteroscedastic model per direction | Location-scale noise model |
| SkewScore [\[33\]](#ref-33) | Skewness of the score function | Heteroscedastic symmetric noise |
| Adaptation speed [\[17\]](#ref-17), [\[19\]](#ref-19) | Steps to re-fit after an intervention | Access to a distribution shift |
| CCA [\[1\]](#ref-1) | Steps for held-out MSE to reach $$\tau$$ | ANM, floor inequality, equal PL constants |

_Table 6. Bivariate direction methods by the statistic they read. In Regime A, CCA reduces to a thresholded RECI; in Regime B it reads conditioning, which Section 6 shows favors the wrong direction._

Two entries matter here. The adaptation-speed line [\[17\]](#ref-17), [\[19\]](#ref-19) is the closest prior work and I should have cited it; the difference is that it measures re-fitting under a distribution shift, not fitting from scratch. And CGNN [\[27\]](#ref-27) already trained one network per direction and compared them, by a distributional fit rather than a step count. The convergence-time framing is new; the two-networks design is not. The preprint's claim that no prior work had used optimization speed as a direction signal was wrong.

### What the last two years did

Four things stand out in the 2024–2026 papers.

**Beyond additive noise.** The action has moved to location-scale noise models, $$Y = f(X) + g(X)\varepsilon$$, where the cause modulates the effect's variance. Immer et al. proved identifiability and gave a maximum-likelihood estimator [\[30\]](#ref-30). Xu et al. then showed that likelihood-based selection breaks when the noise family is misspecified, and that "about 40% of the real-world datasets" in Tübingen exhibit a *misleading conditional variance*, meaning the anticausal conditional spread is the smaller one [\[31\]](#ref-31). That number is a ceiling for every floor-based method, RECI and CCA included: on those pairs equation (10) points the wrong way regardless of how you estimate it. Independence testing survives misspecification better; so does the Student-$$t$$ estimator ROCHE [\[32\]](#ref-32) and the score-skewness criterion of SkewScore [\[33\]](#ref-33). The June 2026 extension handles hidden variables under LSNMs via bow-free mixed graphs [\[34\]](#ref-34).

**Quantile shape instead of noise models.** bQCD compared quantile-conditional variances in 2020 [\[28\]](#ref-28). The September 2025 QPE paper assumes only that the quantile partial effect lies in a finite linear span, an observational-level condition with no mechanism, noise or even Markov assumption, and proves identifiability from that [\[15\]](#ref-15). The September 2026 LRQS paper assumes a monotone transform of the conditional quantile surface is low-rank in the causal direction [\[29\]](#ref-29). Both subsume LSNMs.

**Uncertainty and abstention as first-class outputs.** Causal Discovery via Statistical Power, May 2026, defines *directional power* and returns a bootstrap probability that the direction is right; it also screens out about 34 near-linear Tübingen pairs before deciding [\[35\]](#ref-35). A September 2026 hypothesis-testing framework makes "inconclusive" an official outcome with a consistency proof and confidence intervals for outcome rates [\[36\]](#ref-36). A Bayesian copula method for gene-regulatory direction returns a posterior over the directional contrast and a principled no-call [\[37\]](#ref-37). A direction method needs an abstention and a confidence. The preprint's score had neither.

**Amortized inference.** CDFM, July 2026, and DCD-PFN, June 2026, pretrain transformers on synthetic SCMs in the prior-data-fitted-network style and read causal structure off a dataset in one forward pass [\[38\]](#ref-38), [\[39\]](#ref-39). CDFM reports 67.1% on 95 Tübingen pairs, the same band as the 2018 regression-error ratio.

Two further notes from the sources. RDMDL, at CLeaR 2026, argues that MDL methods had been leaving the description length of the *cause* out of the comparison and introduces a rate-distortion estimate of it; the same paper points out that Tübingen has more discrete causes than effects, an artifact any method can accidentally exploit, and proposes a new benchmark, Lisbon, as the more reliable reference [\[26\]](#ref-26). And a NeurIPS 2025 paper shows standard ANM methods fail under unobserved mediators and proposes a denoising-diffusion estimator that survives them [\[40\]](#ref-40).

## The Tübingen benchmark, done properly

The Tübingen cause-effect pairs [\[41\]](#ref-41) are the standard real-data test: 108 variable pairs from meteorology, biology, economics and engineering with ground-truth direction from domain knowledge. The preprint reported 96% accuracy on 108 pairs. Its Figure 5 shows why that number is not real.

![Tübingen score distribution and confidence-accuracy curve from the preprint](/assets/img/cca/fig_tuebingen_final.png)
_Figure 11. From the preprint. Left: the histogram of mean CCA scores over 108 pairs, about 102 of them within $$\pm 50$$ of zero. Right: cumulative accuracy over pairs sorted from most to least confident; it starts near 0.4 and climbs to 0.96 as the zero-score pairs are added._

The confident predictions are wrong about as often as right. The accuracy comes from the zero-score pairs, which the score did not decide.

So I ran the benchmark under Mooij et al.'s protocol [\[41\]](#ref-41): exclude the six multivariate pairs (52 to 55, 71, 105), leaving 102; weight each pair by the benchmark's weights so that near-duplicate pairs from one source do not dominate; randomly flip the orientation of every pair before the method sees it, so column order carries nothing; three seeds per pair, up to 2000 points per pair, $$T_{\max} = 4000$$; abstentions counted separately; bootstrap confidence intervals over pairs. Same network, same optimizer, same trajectory recording as Section 6. A rule that always answers "first column causes second" scores 53.5% weighted, which confirms the flipping worked.

| Rule | Decides | Weighted accuracy | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preprint rule, $$\tau = 0.05$$ | 5.9% of pairs (6 of 102) | 74.7% (5 of 6) | 27 to 100 |
| Floor comparison, which direction reaches lower | 100% | 60.1% | 47 to 72 |
| Speed at a common reachable threshold, $$1.1 \times$$ the higher floor | 80% | 52.3% | 37 to 66 |
| Speed, $$t_{90}$$: which direction gets there sooner | 99% | 45.7% | 33 to 58 |

![Tübingen decision-rate curves](/assets/img/cca/tuebingen_drc.png)

A held-out MSE below 0.05 on z-scored variables means explaining 95% of the variance, and only five percent of real pairs are that deterministic in either direction. Both networks hit the cap on the other 95%, the score is zero, and the decision fell to whatever broke the tie. Under the protocol the rule abstains on 94% of the benchmark; on the rest its confidence interval runs from 27% to 100%. That is where the 96% came from.

Speed carries nothing on real data. The $$t_{90}$$ rule, the title of this post made operational, scores 45.7%, consistent with Section 6. The floor comparison scores 60.1%, which is where the regression-error family lands in independent benchmarks, and the 40% misleading-conditional-variance ceiling from Xu et al. [\[31\]](#ref-31) is visible in it: the floor rule is wrong on half the pairs unweighted.

## The thermodynamic intuition, revised

The preprint compared $$Y = f(X) + \varepsilon$$ to an irreversible physical process: easy to run forward, expensive to invert. The analogy predicted the wrong observable. Irreversibility does not appear as slow optimization. It appears as an unreachable floor, $$E[\mathrm{Var}(X \mid Y)]$$: information the noise destroyed cannot be recovered by training. Reaching that floor is easy, because there is less structure left to learn.

## What is still open

**Conditioning.** The reverse landscape is better conditioned on five of six mechanisms. Is that general, and can it be predicted from $$f$$? The spectral-bias argument [\[21\]](#ref-21) says the answer depends on the frequency content of $$E[X \mid Y]$$ relative to $$f$$, which is computable. The $$\tanh$$ exception, where the reverse target has a steep region near $$y = \pm 1$$, is consistent with that.

**A threshold is the wrong knob.** Everything in Figure 5 depends on where $$\tau$$ sits relative to two unknown floors. The floors themselves are the quantity of interest, and a good direction score should be a calibrated comparison of them with an abstention rule, in the spirit of the 2026 inference-based methods [\[35\]](#ref-35), [\[36\]](#ref-36).

**Heteroscedasticity.** Equation (1) assumes constant-variance additive noise. On location-scale data the floor comparison can point the wrong way [\[31\]](#ref-31), and 40% of the real benchmark looks like that.

**Multivariate mechanisms.** Everything here is scalar. With vector inputs the floors become traces of conditional covariance matrices, z-scoring may need to become whitening, and injectivity is a different question.

## Beyond two variables: the CCL design

The preprint embedded the direction score in a larger objective it called Causal Compression Learning. The design is worth keeping even though the score inside it has to change. Recover an undirected skeleton with PC-stable [\[42\]](#ref-42), which is order-independent and needs $$n = O(\log(\lvert V \rvert^2/\delta)/\alpha^2)$$ samples for skeleton recovery at minimum partial correlation $$\alpha$$. Orient each edge with a bivariate score. Let a score-based search, GES [\[43\]](#ref-43) or its faster variants, resolve conflicts against an MDL or BIC complexity penalty [\[44\]](#ref-44), with the orientation score entering as a bonus term $$\mathrm{Score}(G) = \mathrm{MDL}(G) + \lambda_3 \cdot \mathrm{Dir}(G)$$. The preprint added a causal information bottleneck [\[45\]](#ref-45), which replaces $$I(T;Y)$$ with $$I_c(Y \mid \mathrm{do}(T))$$ so that confounded features are not retained, and a reinforcement-learning term after Bareinboim, Zhang and Lee [\[46\]](#ref-46).

Its one experiment was a three-variable system, $$X_1 \to X_2 \to X_3$$ with $$X_1 \to X_3$$, $$X_2 = X_1^2 + \varepsilon_1$$, $$X_3 = X_2 + 0.5X_1 + \varepsilon_2$$, sweeping the MDL weight:

| $$\lambda_2$$ | Monotone | $$\mathcal{L}$$ initial | $$\mathcal{L}$$ final | Iterations | Spurious edges |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.01 | yes | 10.000 | $$-1.238$$ | 3 | 1 |
| 0.05 | yes | 10.000 | $$-1.106$$ | 3 | 1 |
| 0.10 | yes | 10.000 | $$-0.942$$ | 3 | 1 |
| 0.20 | yes | 10.000 | $$-0.612$$ | 3 | 1 |
| 0.50 | yes | 10.000 | $$+0.105$$ | 1 | 0 |

_Table 9. From the preprint: the alternating loop on a three-variable SCM. The spurious edge that persists until $$\lambda_2 = 0.5$$ is the $$X_1 \to X_2$$ edge, whose mechanism is the non-injective $$X^2$$._

Two things follow from this post. The persistent spurious edge is the plateau-rule artifact of Section 8 propagating into the graph search; with a threshold rule that edge is an abstention and the search falls back on MDL alone. And the orientation term $$\mathrm{Dir}(G)$$ should be the floor comparison with an abstention band, not a step count. The theorems the preprint stated for this framework, on faithfulness preservation under compression, sample complexity in terms of the minimal I-map edge count, and convergence of the four-term objective, are sketches that chain citations rather than derivations, the reinforcement-learning component was never exercised, and I am not restating them until they are either proved or tested.

## Where a direction test would matter, and what each domain needs

**Medicine.** Whether a biomarker causes a disease or the disease raises the biomarker decides which one to target. Biological mechanisms saturate and have thresholds, so non-injectivity is the norm; with a threshold rule that means abstention rather than error, but it also means low coverage. The 40% misleading-variance rate on Tübingen is a warning about what real biology does to a floor comparison.

**Economics.** Education and earnings, minimum wage and employment. Instrumental variables need an instrument; a bivariate test needs an approximately injective nonlinear mechanism in the observed range and an abstention when it is near-linear, which is what CDSP's screening step does [\[35\]](#ref-35).

**Gene regulation.** Prioritizing which regulatory edges to perturb before a CRISPR screen. The DREAM5 benchmark study [\[37\]](#ref-37) is the model for how to evaluate this honestly: cross-network, small-sample, with no-calls.

**Climate.** Feedback loops between CO$$_2$$ and temperature are the case the static ANM does not cover. Time-series extensions are a separate literature.

The common thread: the bottleneck is not the principle that the causal direction has the lower floor, which is Theorem 2 and holds up. It is coverage. A method that abstains on 94% of real pairs is not yet a tool.

## Toward rung three

Counterfactuals need the abduction-action-prediction cycle [\[2\]](#ref-2): infer the noise that produced the observation, modify the model, predict. Twin networks run the factual and counterfactual worlds side by side sharing that noise. All of it needs the graph, and the graph needs the edges oriented. Nothing about rung three changes if the orientation score changes from a step count to a floor comparison; it only becomes more likely to be right.

## Summary

Training a network from cause to effect reaches a lower loss floor than training from effect to cause. That is Theorem 2, Blöbaum et al. [\[18\]](#ref-18), on top of the identifiability results of Hoyer et al. and Peters et al. [\[5\]](#ref-5), [\[6\]](#ref-6). Training from effect to cause is, on most mechanisms, faster. The preprint measured the first fact through a threshold that sat between the two floors and attributed it to the second.

The causal direction reaches lower. It does not get there sooner.
